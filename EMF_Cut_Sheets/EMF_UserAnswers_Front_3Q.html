Here ρ is the radial distance from the z-axis, φ is the angle measured from the +x-axis in the xy-plane, and z is unchanged. ★ Ans: Transform vectors from Cartesian to Cylindrical coordinate system Introduction: Cartesian coordinates are (x, y, z), while cylindrical coordinates are (ρ, φ, z). Here ρ is the radial distance from the z-axis, φ is the angle measured from the +x-axis in the xy-plane, and z is unchanged. ★ Coordinate relations: x = ρ cosφ; y = ρ sinφ; z = z; ρ = √(x² + y²); φ = tan(-1)(y/x); z = z. ★ Unit vectors: aρ = ax cosφ + ay sinφ; aφ = −ax sinφ + ay cosφ; az = az. ★ Vector transformation: Let A = Ax ax + Ay ay + Az az. Substituting the unit-vector relations: Aρ = Ax cosφ + Ay sinφ; Aφ = −Ax sinφ + Ay cosφ; Az = Az. Therefore A = Aρ aρ + Aφ aφ + Az az. ★ Reverse transformation: Ax = Aρ cosφ − Aφ sinφ; Ay = Aρ sinφ + Aφ cosφ; Az = Az. ★ Conclusion: These relations convert both position and vector components between Cartesian and cylindrical systems. Cylindrical coordinates are useful for problems involving long wires, coaxial cables and cylindrical conductors.

Hence A = Ar ar + Aθ aθ + Aφ aφ. ★ Conclusion: These equations transform vector components from Cartesian to spherical coordinates. Spherical coordinates are especially useful for point charges, spheres and fields having radial symmetry.

[U2·Q1] Q. Explain the point form of Ohm's law Ohm's law states that current through a conductor is proportional to the applied voltage when physical conditions remain constant. Its microscopic or point form relates current density to electric field intensity at each point in a conducting medium. ★ Ans: Explain the point form of Ohm's law Ohm's law states that current through a conductor is proportional to the applied voltage when physical conditions remain constant. Its microscopic or point form relates current density to electric field intensity at each point in a conducting medium. ★ Point form: J = σE or E = ρJ; where J is current density (A/m²), E is electric field intensity (V/m), σ is conductivity (S/m), and ρ = 1/σ is resistivity (Ω m). ★ Derivation idea: For a uniform conductor of length l and cross-sectional area A, V = El and I = JA. From macroscopic Ohm's law V = IR, and R = ρl/A. Substitution gives El = (JA)(ρl/A) = ρJl. Thus E = ρJ, or J = σE. ★ Meaning: Current density is directly proportional to electric field. A material with high conductivity carries more current density for the same electric field. The relation is valid for an ohmic, isotropic material under conditions where σ is constant. ★ Applications: It is used in conduction, resistance calculations, current distribution and electromagnetic field analysis.
[U2·Q2] Q. Explain the point form of continuity equation The continuity equation expresses conservation of electric charge. It states that the net current flowing out of a small volume is equal to the rate of decrease of charge stored inside that volume. ★ Ans: Explain the point form of continuity equation The continuity equation expresses conservation of electric charge. It states that the net current flowing out of a small volume is equal to the rate of decrease of charge stored inside that volume. ★ Integral form: ∮S J·dS = −d/dt ∫V ρv dV. Using the divergence theorem, ∫V (∇·J)dV = −∫V (∂ρv/∂t)dV. Since the volume is arbitrary: ★ Point form: ∇·J = −∂ρv/∂t. Here J is current density and ρv is volume charge density. For steady current, charge density does not change with time, so ∂ρv/∂t = 0 and ∇·J = 0. ★ Physical significance: Charge cannot be created or destroyed in an ordinary conducting system. If more current leaves a region than enters it, the charge stored in that region decreases. If more current enters, charge accumulates. ★ Applications: The equation is used in circuit theory, current flow, semiconductor devices and Maxwell's electromagnetic field equations.

[U3·Q1] Q. Here H is magnetic field intensity (A/m), dl is the differential length vector along the closed path, J is current density and Ienc is the current passing through the surface bounded by the path. ★ Explanation: Choose a closed Amperian path around a current-carrying conductor. The magnetic field circulates around the current. The line integral adds the tangential component of H along the complete path. The direction is determined by the right-hand rule. ★ Applications: It is useful for finding magnetic fields in highly symmetrical cases, such as an infinitely long straight conductor, a solenoid and a toroid. ★ Point form for magnetostatics: ∇×H = J. For time-varying fields, Maxwell adds displacement current: ∇×H = J + ∂D/∂t. ★ Conclusion: Ampere's law relates magnetic field intensity to the current producing it.
[U3·Q2] Q. Derive scalar magnetic potential In a current-free region of a magnetostatic field, the current density is zero: J = 0. Ampere's law in point form gives ∇×H = J = 0. Thus H is an irrotational field in that region. ★ Ans: Derive scalar magnetic potential In a current-free region of a magnetostatic field, the current density is zero: J = 0. Ampere's law in point form gives ∇×H = J = 0. Thus H is an irrotational field in that region. ★ A curl-free vector field can be expressed as the negative gradient of a scalar function. Therefore: H = −∇Vm; where Vm is called scalar magnetic potential, measured in ampere-turns or A in the usual magnetic-potential convention. ★ In Cartesian coordinates: Hx = −∂Vm/∂x; Hy = −∂Vm/∂y; Hz = −∂Vm/∂z. The magnetic flux density is B = μH = −μ∇Vm for a linear medium. Taking divergence and using ∇·B = 0 gives ∇·(μ∇Vm) = 0. For constant permeability, ∇²Vm = 0. ★ Important limitation: Scalar magnetic potential is defined conveniently in current-free regions. If a path encloses current, the scalar potential may be multivalued and cannot be used as a single-valued global potential. ★ Conclusion: Scalar magnetic potential simplifies magnetic-field calculations in current-free regions, in a manner similar to electric potential in electrostatics.

[U4·Q1] Q. Derive displacement current through a capacitor Consider a parallel-plate capacitor connected to a time-varying voltage source. During charging, conduction current flows in the wires, but no conduction current passes through the dielectric gap. However, the changing electric field between the plates produces displacement current. ★ Ans: Derive displacement current through a capacitor Consider a parallel-plate capacitor connected to a time-varying voltage source. During charging, conduction current flows in the wires, but no conduction current passes through the dielectric gap. However, the changing electric field between the plates produces displacement current. ★ For plate area A, electric flux density is D = εE. The electric flux through the plate area is Ψ = ∫S D·dS. The displacement current is defined as the time rate of change of electric flux: Id = d/dt ∫S D·dS. ★ For a uniform field, Id = A(∂D/∂t) = εA(∂E/∂t). Since E = V/d, Id = (εA/d)(dV/dt). The capacitance is C = εA/d, so: Id = C(dV/dt). ★ In current-density form, Jd = ∂D/∂t. The displacement current has the same magnitude as the conduction current in the connecting wire for an ideal charging capacitor. ★ Importance: Maxwell introduced displacement current to maintain current continuity and to correct Ampere's law for time-varying fields.
[U4·Q2] Q. Explain Maxwell's first equation (Gauss's law for electric fields) Maxwell's first equation states that the total electric flux leaving a closed surface is equal to the total free charge enclosed by that surface. ★ Ans: Explain Maxwell's first equation (Gauss's law for electric fields) Maxwell's first equation states that the total electric flux leaving a closed surface is equal to the total free charge enclosed by that surface. ★ Integral form: ∮S D·dS = Qenc = ∫V ρv dV. ★ Point form: ∇·D = ρv. Here D is electric flux density (C/m²), ρv is volume charge density (C/m³), and S is a closed surface enclosing volume V. ★ Derivation: Start with Gauss's law, ∮S D·dS = ∫V ρv dV. Applying the divergence theorem to the left side gives ∫V (∇·D)dV = ∫V ρv dV. Since the volume is arbitrary, the integrands are equal: ∇·D = ρv. ★ Physical meaning: Electric charges are the sources or sinks of electric flux. Positive charge produces outward flux, while negative charge produces inward flux. The net flux through a closed surface depends only on the enclosed charge. ★ In a linear medium: D = εE, so ∇·E = ρv/ε for constant ε. Applications: It is used to determine electric fields and charge distributions.

For time-varying fields, Maxwell added displacement current to the original law. ★ Integral form: ∮C H·dl = ∫S J·dS + ∂/∂t ∫S D·dS. ★ Point form: ∇×H = J + ∂D/∂t. Here H is magnetic field intensity, J is conduction-current density and ∂D/∂t is displacement-current density. ★ Explanation: Conduction current is due to movement of free charges in a conductor. In a charging capacitor, conduction current flows in the wires and displacement current exists in the dielectric gap. ★ For steady fields: ∂D/∂t = 0, so ∇×H = J, which is the magnetostatic form. ★ Importance: Maxwell's correction makes Ampere's law valid for time-varying fields and is essential for predicting electromagnetic waves.
[U5·Q1] Q. Derive the wave equation for a perfect dielectric in free space A perfect dielectric is a medium with zero conductivity (σ = 0), no free charge in the propagation region, and constant permittivity ε and permeability μ. In a source-free region, Maxwell's equations are: ∇×E = −μ ∂H/∂t; ∇×H = ε ∂E/∂t; ∇·E = 0 and ∇·H = 0. ★ Ans: Derive the wave equation for a perfect dielectric in free space A perfect dielectric is a medium with zero conductivity (σ = 0), no free charge in the propagation region, and constant permittivity ε and permeability μ. In a source-free region, Maxwell's equations are: ∇×E = −μ ∂H/∂t; ∇×H = ε ∂E/∂t; ∇·E = 0 and ∇·H = 0. ★ Derivation for E: Take curl on both sides of Faraday's law: ∇×(∇×E) = −μ ∂/∂t(∇×H). Use the identity ∇×(∇×E) = ∇(∇·E) − ∇²E. Since ∇·E = 0, the left side is −∇²E. Substitute ∇×H = ε∂E/∂t: −∇²E = −με ∂²E/∂t². ★ Therefore: ∇²E = με ∂²E/∂t². ★ Derivation for H: Similarly, take curl of ∇×H = ε∂E/∂t and use Faraday's law: ∇²H = με ∂²H/∂t². ★ These are the electromagnetic wave equations. Wave velocity is v = 1/√(με). In free space, v = c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s. ★ Conclusion: Electric and magnetic fields propagate as waves in a perfect dielectric, with E, H and direction mutually perpendicular.
[U5·Q2] Q. Derive the wave equation for a conducting medium Consider a homogeneous conducting medium with conductivity σ, permittivity ε and permeability μ. In a source-free region, the Maxwell equations are: ∇×E = −μ∂H/∂t; ∇×H = σE + ε∂E/∂t. ★ Ans: Derive the wave equation for a conducting medium Consider a homogeneous conducting medium with conductivity σ, permittivity ε and permeability μ. In a source-free region, the Maxwell equations are: ∇×E = −μ∂H/∂t; ∇×H = σE + ε∂E/∂t. ★ Derivation for electric field: Take curl of Faraday's law: ∇×(∇×E) = −μ∂/∂t(∇×H). Using ∇×(∇×E) = ∇(∇·E) − ∇²E and assuming ∇·E = 0: −∇²E = −μ[σ∂E/∂t + ε∂²E/∂t²]. ★ Therefore: ∇²E = μσ∂E/∂t + με∂²E/∂t². ★ Magnetic-field wave equation: ∇²H = μσ∂H/∂t + με∂²H/∂t². The first term on the right represents conductivity and causes attenuation; the second term represents wave propagation. For σ = 0, the equations reduce to perfect-dielectric equations. ★ Conclusion: In a conducting medium, electromagnetic waves travel with attenuation because part of field energy is dissipated as heat. The amount of attenuation depends on σ, μ, ε and frequency.
[U5·Q3] Q. Derive Poynting vector from Maxwell's equations for the general case Poynting's theorem expresses conservation of electromagnetic energy. Start with Maxwell's equations: ∇×H = J + ∂D/∂t and ∇×E = −∂B/∂t. ★ Ans: Derive Poynting vector from Maxwell's equations for the general case Poynting's theorem expresses conservation of electromagnetic energy. Start with Maxwell's equations: ∇×H = J + ∂D/∂t and ∇×E = −∂B/∂t. ★ Dot the first equation with E: E·(∇×H) = E·J + E·∂D/∂t. Dot the second equation with H: H·(∇×E) = −H·∂B/∂t. ★ Use the vector identity ∇·(E×H) = H·(∇×E) − E·(∇×H). Combining the two equations: ∇·(E×H) = −E·J − E·∂D/∂t − H·∂B/∂t. ★ For a linear medium, stored electric and magnetic energy densities are we = ½E·D and wm = ½B·H. Thus: ∇·(E×H) = −J·E − ∂(½E·D + ½B·H)/∂t. ★ Integral form: ∮S (E×H)·dS = −∫V J·E dv − d/dt∫V (½E·D + ½B·H)dV. ★ Where S = E×H is the Poynting vector, measured in W/m². It gives direction and rate of electromagnetic energy flow per unit area. The theorem states that power flowing out of a closed surface equals decrease in stored field energy minus power delivered to charges.
